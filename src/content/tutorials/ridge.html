Ridge Regression from Scratch

A complete walkthrough of Hoerl & Kennard, "Ridge Regression: Biased Estimation for Nonorthogonal Problems", Technometrics 12(1), 1970

This tutorial works through Hoerl & Kennard's paper itself, and it starts where Session 0 stopped: with least squares proved optimal by Gauss–Markov, and about to be dethroned. Every result in the paper is derived here, with the original equation numbers in the margin so you can read the two side by side.

This is Session 1 — do Session 0 first

Everything below assumes Least Squares Foundations, which builds the following from scratch. If any of it looks unfamiliar, start there.

  • The model \(Y = X\beta + \varepsilon\), and the two noise assumptions \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]=0\), \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\varepsilon']=\sigma^2 I\).
  • \(X'X\) as the correlation matrix, and eigenvalues as the spread of the data cloud in each direction.
  • Least squares: \(\hat\beta = (X'X)^{-1}X'Y\), unbiased, with \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\beta) = \sigma^2(X'X)^{-1}\).
  • Bias, variance, and the split \(\text{MSE} = \text{variance} + \text{bias}^2\).
  • The Gauss–Markov theorem: least squares is best among linear unbiased estimators.

That last line is the one the paper attacks. Keep both qualifiers in mind.

How the argument goes

The whole paper tracks one number: \(\mathbb{E}[L_1^2]\), the expected squared distance from the estimate to the true \(\beta\). Five moves:

  1. Measure the damage. For least squares \(\mathbb{E}[L_1^2] = \sigma^2\sum_i 1/\lambda_i\), which explodes when predictors are correlated.
  2. Propose the fix. Add \(kI\) before inverting, and work out what that does to each direction.
  3. Justify the fix. Among all estimates that fit the data equally well, it is the shortest one.
  4. Prove it works. Split \(\mathbb{E}[L_1^2]\) into variance plus squared bias, and show some \(k\) always beats least squares.
  5. Choose \(k\). The ridge trace.

And one tool throughout. Every formula is rewritten along the eigenvector directions of \(X'X\). The reason is practical: a matrix expression cannot be judged big or small, but the same quantity written as a sum over directions can be differentiated and sign-checked one term at a time. That is why \(\lambda_i\) keeps reappearing, and why we keep changing coordinates — the two theorems in step 4 are proved term by term, and there are no terms without it.

Section 1 · paper §1, eqs (1.4)–(1.9)

Where least squares breaks

Least squares is unbiased and, by Gauss–Markov, has the smallest variance of any linear unbiased estimator. Session 0 established both. This section is where Hoerl and Kennard show that neither fact saves you.

The question is simple: how far is \(\hat\beta\) from \(\beta\), on average? We need the error in a usable form first. Throughout, write \(A = (X'X)^{-1}X'\) for the constant matrix in front of the noise.

Derivation · putting the error in quadratic form
  1. from Session 0: \(\;\hat\beta = \beta + A\varepsilon\), so the error is \(\;\hat\beta - \beta = A\varepsilon\)
  2. the paper names its squared length \(L_1^2\): \(\;L_1^2 \equiv (\hat\beta-\beta)'(\hat\beta-\beta) = (A\varepsilon)'(A\varepsilon)\)
  3. \((A\varepsilon)' = \varepsilon'A'\), so \(\;L_1^2 = \varepsilon' A'A\, \varepsilon\)
  4. \(A'A = X(X'X)^{-1}(X'X)^{-1}X' = X(X'X)^{-2}X'\), using that \((X'X)^{-1}\) is symmetric
  5. \(\;\;L_1^2 = \varepsilon'\,X(X'X)^{-2}X'\,\varepsilon\) — a quadratic form in the noise
Everything random now sits in \(\varepsilon\); the matrix in the middle is fixed. Abbreviate it \(M = X(X'X)^{-2}X'\), so \(L_1^2 = \varepsilon'M\varepsilon\).
What is a "quadratic form"? Any expression shaped \(v'Mv\) — a vector, a square matrix, the same vector again. It returns a single number in which every term has degree two in the entries of \(v\); it is the multi-dimensional version of \(ax^2\). In two dimensions \(v'Mv = m_{11}v_1^2 + (m_{12}+m_{21})v_1v_2 + m_{22}v_2^2\). Ours is quadratic in the noise: \(L_1^2 = \sum_i\sum_j M_{ij}\,\varepsilon_i\varepsilon_j\), a weighted sum of every product of two noise components. You have met others already — \(\|v\|^2 = v'Iv\), and the residual sum of squares \(\phi(B)\), which is quadratic in \(B\). Quadratic forms are exactly the functions whose contours are ellipsoids, which is why Section 3 has ellipses in it.

Now take the expectation. The obstacle is that \(\varepsilon\) appears twice, on both sides of \(M\), so we cannot simply pull it out. The standard escape is the trace.

Derivation · the expectation, via the trace (1.5) → (1.8)
  1. \(L_1^2 = \varepsilon'M\varepsilon\) is a single number — a \(1\times1\) matrix — and the trace of a \(1\times1\) matrix is that number:
  2. \(L_1^2 = \mathrm{tr}\big(\varepsilon'M\varepsilon\big)\)
  3. the trace is cyclic, \(\mathrm{tr}(ABC) = \mathrm{tr}(BCA)\); take \(A = \varepsilon'\), \(B = M\), \(C = \varepsilon\):
  4. \(L_1^2 = \mathrm{tr}\big(M\,\varepsilon\varepsilon'\big)\) — the two copies of \(\varepsilon\) are now adjacent
  5. now take expectations of both sides: \(\;\mathbb{E}[L_1^2] = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{tr}(M\varepsilon\varepsilon')\big]\)
  6. the trace is a sum of diagonal entries, and the expectation of a sum is the sum of expectations, so the two operations swap: \(\;\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{tr}(Z)] = \mathrm{tr}(\mathbb{E}[Z])\), giving \(\;= \mathrm{tr}\big(\mathbb{E}[M\varepsilon\varepsilon']\big)\)
  7. \(M\) is built only from \(X\), which is fixed, so it factors out of the expectation just as \(\mathbb{E}[3Z] = 3\mathbb{E}[Z]\): \(\;= \mathrm{tr}\big(M\,\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\varepsilon']\big)\)
  8. substitute the noise assumption \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\varepsilon'] = \sigma^2 I\): \(\;= \mathrm{tr}(M\,\sigma^2 I) = \mathrm{tr}(\sigma^2 M)\)
  9. \(\sigma^2\) is a scalar, so it factors out of the sum of diagonal entries: \(\;= \sigma^2\,\mathrm{tr}(M)\)
  10. unpack \(M\) and cycle once more: \(\;\mathrm{tr}\big(X(X'X)^{-2}X'\big) = \mathrm{tr}\big((X'X)^{-2}X'X\big) = \mathrm{tr}\big((X'X)^{-1}\big)\)
  11. the eigenvalues of an inverse are the reciprocals: if \(Av = \lambda v\) then \(A^{-1}v = (1/\lambda)v\), so \((X'X)^{-1}\) has eigenvalues \(1/\lambda_1,\dots,1/\lambda_p\)
  12. and the trace of any square matrix is the sum of its eigenvalues, so \(\;\mathrm{tr}\big((X'X)^{-1}\big) = \sum_i 1/\lambda_i\), giving
  13. \(\;\;\mathbb{E}[L_1^2] = \sigma^2\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{p} \frac{1}{\lambda_i}\) — equation (1.8)
Prefer to see the last step with \(P\)? Write \(X'X = P\Lambda P'\) with \(P\) orthogonal, so \(P'P = I\). Inverting gives \((X'X)^{-1} = P\Lambda^{-1}P'\), and \(\Lambda^{-1} = \mathrm{diag}(1/\lambda_1,\dots,1/\lambda_p)\) since inverting a diagonal matrix just inverts each entry. Then the cyclic property from step 3 finishes it: \(\mathrm{tr}(P\Lambda^{-1}P') = \mathrm{tr}(\Lambda^{-1}P'P) = \mathrm{tr}(\Lambda^{-1}) = \sum_i 1/\lambda_i\).

Steps 1–4 are the trick worth memorising: a scalar equals its trace, and the trace lets you move the random factors next to each other so a single \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\varepsilon']\) can be substituted. It is how the expectation of any quadratic form is computed, and it reappears in Section 4 when we do the same for ridge.

If the trace manipulation feels like sleight of hand, here is the same result the elementary way. Expand the form as a double sum and push the expectation inside: \(\mathbb{E}[L_1^2] = \sum_{i}\sum_j M_{ij}\,\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_i\varepsilon_j]\). The noise assumption says \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_i\varepsilon_j]\) is \(\sigma^2\) when \(i=j\) and \(0\) otherwise, so every cross-term dies and only the diagonal survives: \(\mathbb{E}[L_1^2] = \sigma^2\sum_i M_{ii} = \sigma^2\,\mathrm{tr}(M)\). Same answer — and it shows why a trace appears at all: the trace is the sum of the diagonal, and the diagonal is precisely what uncorrelated noise leaves behind.

Two companion facts from the same section of the paper. Because every term \(1/\lambda_i\) is positive, the sum is at least its largest term, giving the lower bound

\[\mathbb{E}[L_1^2] \;\ge\; \frac{\sigma^2}{\lambda_{\min}}\]

The paper also records the variance of the error, equations (1.6) and (1.9). That one needs the errors to be normal, and it comes from a representation worth seeing, because the same line delivers (1.8) as well.

Derivation · a weighted chi-square, giving (1.8) and (1.9) together
  1. \(M\) is symmetric, so diagonalise it: \(M = QDQ'\) with \(Q\) orthogonal. Its non-zero eigenvalues are the \(1/\lambda_i\).
  2. set \(u = Q'\varepsilon\). An orthogonal transformation of independent normals is again independent normal, so \(u_i \sim N(0,\sigma^2)\), independent — this is the step that needs normality.
  3. \(L_1^2 = \varepsilon'Q D Q'\varepsilon = u'Du = \displaystyle\sum_i \frac{1}{\lambda_i}\,u_i^2\) — a weighted sum of independent squared normals
  4. each \(u_i^2/\sigma^2\) is \(\chi^2_1\), for which the mean is \(1\) and the variance is \(2\); so \(\mathbb{E}[u_i^2] = \sigma^2\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(u_i^2) = 2\sigma^4\)
  5. means add: \(\;\mathbb{E}[L_1^2] = \sum_i \frac{1}{\lambda_i}\sigma^2 = \sigma^2\sum_i \frac{1}{\lambda_i}\) — equation (1.8) again
  6. variances of independent terms add, and each weight is squared on the way out, \(\mathrm{Var}(cZ) = c^2\mathrm{Var}(Z)\):
  7. \(\;\;\mathrm{Var}[L_1^2] = \sum_i \Big(\frac{1}{\lambda_i}\Big)^{2} 2\sigma^4 = 2\sigma^4\sum_i \Big(\frac{1}{\lambda_i}\Big)^{2}\) — equation (1.9)
Note the asymmetry in step 6: a small \(\lambda_i\) enters the mean as \(1/\lambda_i\) but the variance as \(1/\lambda_i^2\). Halve an eigenvalue and the average error doubles while its spread quadruples. So an ill-conditioned problem is not just wrong on average — it is wildly inconsistent from one dataset to the next, which is exactly what the simulation below shows.

Read equation (1.8) as a warning label. If the predictors were orthogonal, every \(\lambda_i = 1\) and the error would be a modest \(p\sigma^2\). But Session 0 showed that correlation drives some \(\lambda_i\) toward zero — and \(1/\lambda_i\) then explodes.

There is a second symptom. The same sum appears in the expected length of the estimate:

Derivation · the estimate comes out too long (1.5a)
  1. \(\hat\beta'\hat\beta = (\beta + A\varepsilon)'(\beta + A\varepsilon)\)
  2. expand all four products: \(\;= \beta'\beta + \beta'A\varepsilon + (A\varepsilon)'\beta + (A\varepsilon)'(A\varepsilon)\)
  3. the two middle terms are \(1\times1\) and transposes of each other, so they are equal: \(\;= \beta'\beta + 2\beta'A\varepsilon + L_1^2\)
  4. take expectations term by term; \(\beta'\beta\) is constant, and \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0\) kills the middle:
  5. \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\beta'\hat\beta] = \beta'\beta + 2\beta'A\,\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] + \mathbb{E}[L_1^2] = \beta'\beta + \sigma^2\sum_i 1/\lambda_i\)
Note what this says and what it does not. It is not a statement about bias — least squares is still unbiased, and \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\beta] = \beta\) exactly. It is a statement about length: the estimate scatters in all directions, and squared length picks up that scatter no matter which way it goes. So on average the fitted vector is too long, inflated by exactly the quantity from the previous derivation. Practitioners see this as coefficients too large in absolute value, and sometimes so large that they come out with the wrong sign.

A worked example with two predictors

Take \(p = 2\) with correlation \(\rho\), so \(X'X = \begin{pmatrix}1 & \rho \\ \rho & 1\end{pmatrix}\). Its eigenvalues are worth checking by hand, because the two directions have such different meanings:

Derivation · the two eigenvalues of a 2×2 correlation matrix
  1. \(\begin{pmatrix}1 & \rho\\ \rho & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1+\rho\\1+\rho\end{pmatrix} = (1+\rho)\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\) \(\Rightarrow\) \(\lambda = 1+\rho\) along \((1,1)\)
  2. \(\begin{pmatrix}1 & \rho\\ \rho & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1-\rho\\ \rho-1\end{pmatrix} = (1-\rho)\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\) \(\Rightarrow\) \(\lambda = 1-\rho\) along \((1,-1)\)
  3. they sum to \(2 = p\), as the trace demands
The sum direction \((1,1)\) keeps almost all the spread; the difference direction \((1,-1)\) is where correlation drains it away. Since \(\rho\) can approach 1, the second eigenvalue can be driven arbitrarily close to zero.

At \(\rho = 0.99\):

\[\sum_i \frac{1}{\lambda_i} = \frac{1}{1.99} + \frac{1}{0.01} \approx 100.5 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbb{E}[L_1^2] \approx 100\,\sigma^2\]

versus \(2\sigma^2\) if the predictors had been orthogonal. Fifty times worse, from correlation alone.

The eigenvector for the small eigenvalue is \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)\) — the difference of the two coefficients. Since the two predictors are nearly identical, the data has essentially no information about that difference, so least squares fills it with noise: two huge coefficients of opposite sign that cancel out.

Watch it happen

Here is a real simulation. Two predictors with correlation \(0.998\), true coefficients \(\beta = (1.0,\ 2.0)\), and the same model fitted on three different draws of the noise. Only the noise changes between rows.

Noise drawLeast squaresRidge, \(k = 0.3\)
1(−0.8, +3.5)(+1.1, +1.2)
2(+10.3, −7.9)(+1.1, +1.0)
3(+8.5, −5.6)(+1.3, +1.2)
truth(+1.0, +2.0)

The least squares estimates are enormous, they flip sign between draws, and they are essentially noise. The ridge estimates — which we are about to define — barely move.

Notice one honest detail: ridge returns roughly \(1.1\) for both coefficients when the truth is \(1\) and \(2\). The predictors are so alike that the data genuinely cannot tell them apart, so ridge splits the credit. That gap is the bias. But it is a small, predictable error instead of a catastrophic random one.

Why not just drop one of the correlated predictors? The paper answers this in §0. Dropping predictors destroys the model's use for control and optimization — you are left, in their words, with "a set of dangling controllables" — and which one to drop is itself an unstable decision. Ridge keeps every predictor and stabilizes the whole vector at once. (Twenty-six years later, the lasso would make "drop variables" work properly; see Section 6.)
But the estimate is still unbiased — isn't that good? Yes, and that is exactly the point. It is unbiased and on average \(100\sigma^2\) away from the truth. Unbiasedness describes the average of many darts; it promises nothing about the one dart you actually threw.

Section 2 · paper §2, eqs (2.1)–(2.9)

The ridge estimator

The remedy is one line. Before inverting, add a small positive constant \(k\) to every diagonal entry of \(X'X\):

\[\hat\beta^{*}(k) = (X'X + kI)^{-1}X'Y, \qquad k \ge 0 \tag{2.1}\]

That is the whole method. Hoerl had proposed it in 1962; the name comes from "ridge analysis", a technique for studying quadratic response surfaces in chemical engineering.

It is a shrunken version of least squares

Derivation · ridge is a shrunken least squares (2.3)–(2.4)
  1. start from the definition (2.1): \(\;\hat\beta^{*} = (X'X + kI)^{-1}X'Y\)
  2. the normal equations (Session 0) say \(X'Y = X'X\hat\beta\), so substituting:
  3. \(\;\;\hat\beta^{*} = \underbrace{(X'X + kI)^{-1}X'X}_{\textstyle Z}\;\hat\beta \;=\; Z\hat\beta\) — equations (2.3)–(2.4)
Short, but it is the line the rest of the paper leans on: ridge does not compute something new. It takes the least squares answer and multiplies it by a fixed matrix \(Z\), with \(k\) setting the strength. Everything that follows — the shrink factors below, and the whole error decomposition in Section 4 — starts from \(\hat\beta^{*} = Z\hat\beta\).

The limits read straight off it. At \(k = 0\), \(Z = (X'X)^{-1}X'X = I\), so \(\hat\beta^{*} = \hat\beta\) and we are back to least squares. As \(k\) grows, \((X'X+kI)^{-1} \to 0\), so \(Z \to 0\) and the estimate is crushed to nothing. (The paper writes the same \(Z\) in the equivalent form \(\big[I + k(X'X)^{-1}\big]^{-1}\); we will not need that version.)

What it does, direction by direction

Saying \(Z\) "shrinks" the estimate is vague, because \(Z\) is a matrix and matrices can rotate as well as scale. So look at what it does along each eigen-direction of \(X'X\).

Derivation · the shrink factors (2.5)–(2.6)
  1. adding \(kI\) shifts every eigenvalue and moves no eigenvector: if \((X'X)v = \lambda v\) then \(\;(X'X + kI)v = \lambda v + kv = (\lambda+k)v\)
  2. so the same \(P\) diagonalises both, and \(\;X'X + kI = P(\Lambda + kI)P'\)
  3. therefore \(\;Z = (X'X+kI)^{-1}X'X = P(\Lambda+kI)^{-1}P'\cdot P\Lambda P' = P\,(\Lambda+kI)^{-1}\Lambda\,P'\), using \(P'P = I\)
  4. that middle factor is diagonal — inverting a diagonal takes reciprocals, and diagonals multiply entry by entry. Call it \(D\):
  5. \(\;\;D = (\Lambda+kI)^{-1}\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}\Big(\dfrac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i+k}\Big)\), every entry in \((0,1)\) since \(0 \lt \lambda_i \lt \lambda_i+k\)
  6. so \(\;\;Z = PDP'\)
\(Z\) does not rotate anything: it keeps \(X'X\)'s eigenvectors and rescales the \(i\)-th by \(\lambda_i/(\lambda_i+k)\). Step 1 is the reason adding \(kI\) is a sensible move at all — a multiple of the identity stretches every direction equally and turns none, so the dangerous \(1/\lambda_{\min}\) becomes \(1/(\lambda_{\min}+k)\), which cannot blow up however correlated the predictors are.

So \(Z\) stretches each of the two directions by its own factor. But \(\hat\beta\) is not one of those directions — it is a combination of them. So split it into its two parts, one along each direction:

\[\hat\beta \;=\; \hat\alpha_1 v_1 \;+\; \hat\alpha_2 v_2\]

The numbers \(\hat\alpha_1\) and \(\hat\alpha_2\) are simply the lengths of those two parts — how far \(\hat\beta\) reaches along each direction. Ridge then multiplies each part by its own factor and adds them back up:

\[\hat\beta^{*} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+k}}_{\text{near }1}\hat\alpha_1 v_1 \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_2+k}}_{\text{near }0}\hat\alpha_2 v_2\]

That is the entire operation: split, scale each part, add back. The matrix bookkeeping for it — collecting the lengths as \(\hat\alpha = P'\hat\beta\) and the factors as \(D\), so that \(\hat\alpha^{*} = D\hat\alpha\) — is just a compact way of writing those two lines.

The method in one line\[\hat\alpha_i^{*} = \frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i + k}\,\hat\alpha_i\]Where \(\lambda_i\) is large the factor is near 1 — the data spoke clearly in that direction, so leave the estimate alone. Where \(\lambda_i\) is near zero the factor is near 0 — the data was silent, so shrink the estimate away rather than let noise fill it.

Put numbers on it. With \(\rho = 0.99\) the eigenvalues are \(\lambda_1 = 1.99\) and \(\lambda_2 = 0.01\); take \(k = 0.1\). The "sum" direction keeps \(1.99/2.09 \approx 95\%\) of its value, while the "difference" direction keeps \(0.01/0.11 \approx 9\%\). Ridge barely touches the direction the data measured well and all but deletes the one it could not see — which is why the estimates in Section 1 stopped swinging.

Derivation · ridge is always shorter (2.8)
  1. work in the eigenvector basis again. Rotating by the orthogonal \(P\) changes no lengths, so \(\;\|\hat\beta^{*}\|^2 = \|\hat\alpha^{*}\|^2\) and \(\|\hat\beta\|^2 = \|\hat\alpha\|^2\)
  2. the previous derivation gave each coordinate: \(\;\hat\alpha_i^{*} = \dfrac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i+k}\,\hat\alpha_i\)
  3. so \(\;\|\hat\beta^{*}\|^2 = \displaystyle\sum_i \Big(\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i+k}\Big)^{2}\hat\alpha_i^{\,2}\)
  4. for \(k \gt 0\) every factor obeys \(0 \lt \dfrac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i+k} \lt 1\), so each term is strictly smaller than \(\hat\alpha_i^{\,2}\)
  5. \(\;\;\|\hat\beta^{*}\|^2 \lt \displaystyle\sum_i \hat\alpha_i^{\,2} = \|\hat\beta\|^2\) — equation (2.8)
Every coordinate shrinks, so the whole vector shrinks. That is the entire argument, and it needs nothing beyond the shrink factors we already have. Two footnotes. The inequality is strict provided \(\hat\beta \ne 0\) — if it were zero there would be nothing to shrink. And the largest shrink factor is \(\lambda_1/(\lambda_1+k)\), because \(\lambda/(\lambda+k) = 1 - k/(\lambda+k)\) grows with \(\lambda\); that quantity is what the paper calls \(\xi_{\max}(Z)\) in equation (2.9), \(\xi\) being its letter for the eigenvalues of \(Z\).

This is why ridge is called a shrinkage method — and it is also the clue to the geometry in the next section.

Section 3 · paper §3b, eqs (3.1)–(3.7)

The geometry: shortest good explanation

Adding \(kI\) works, but it looks arbitrary. Why this fix? The paper answers with a characterization that makes the formula inevitable. First, what do the contours of the error function look like?

Derivation · error contours are ellipsoids around \(\hat\beta\) (3.1)
  1. take any candidate \(B\) and split the residual around \(\hat\beta\): \(\;Y - XB = (Y - X\hat\beta) + X(\hat\beta - B)\)
  2. name the two pieces \(r = Y - X\hat\beta\) (the least squares residual) and \(u = X(\hat\beta - B)\), so \(\;\phi(B) = (r+u)'(r+u)\)
  3. expand the square: \(\;= r'r + 2r'u + u'u\), the two middle terms being equal because each is \(1\times1\) and the transpose of the other
  4. the first piece is the best achievable error: \(\;r'r = \phi(\hat\beta) = \phi_{\min}\)
  5. the third piece is \(\;u'u = (\hat\beta-B)'X'X(\hat\beta-B) = (B-\hat\beta)'X'X(B-\hat\beta)\), the sign cancelling since it appears twice
  6. the cross term dies: \(\;2r'u = 2\big[X'(Y - X\hat\beta)\big]'(\hat\beta - B)\), and \(X'(Y-X\hat\beta) = X'Y - X'X\hat\beta = 0\) by the normal equations
  7. \(\;\;\phi(B) = \phi_{\min} + (B-\hat\beta)'X'X(B-\hat\beta)\) — equation (3.1)
Read the result as a statement about shape. The error of any candidate is the unavoidable minimum plus a quadratic form in how far that candidate sits from \(\hat\beta\). Setting it equal to a constant therefore carves out an ellipsoid centred on \(\hat\beta\), with axes along the eigenvectors of \(X'X\) and half-lengths proportional to \(1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}\). Small-\(\lambda\) directions give long, thin ellipsoids — you can travel a long way in those directions while the fit barely worsens, which is exactly the freedom ridge is about to exploit.

That last observation suggests a question. If many different coefficient vectors explain the data equally well, which should we choose? The paper's answer: the shortest one.

Derivation · minimum length on a contour (3.2)–(3.5)
  1. the problem: minimize the length \(\;B'B\;\) subject to staying on one contour, \(\;(B-\hat\beta)'X'X(B-\hat\beta) = \phi_0\)
  2. build the Lagrangian — objective plus multiplier times constraint. Writing the multiplier as \(1/k\) rather than the usual \(\mu\) is a choice that pays off in two lines:
  3. \(F(B) = B'B + \tfrac{1}{k}\big[(B-\hat\beta)'X'X(B-\hat\beta) - \phi_0\big]\)
  4. differentiate. The first term gives \(\partial(B'B)/\partial B = 2B\); for the second, \(\partial\big[(B-c)'A(B-c)\big]/\partial B = 2A(B-c)\) for symmetric \(A\), here \(A = X'X\) and \(c = \hat\beta\):
  5. \(\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial B} = 2B + \dfrac{2}{k}X'X(B - \hat\beta) = 0\)
  6. multiply through by \(k/2\): \(\;kB + X'XB - X'X\hat\beta = 0\)
  7. collect the \(B\) terms: \(\;(X'X + kI)\,B = X'X\hat\beta = X'Y\), the last equality being the normal equations
  8. \(\;\;B = (X'X + kI)^{-1}X'Y = \hat\beta^{*}\) — equation (3.5); the ridge estimator falls out
A Lagrange multiplier is the standard tool for optimising under a constraint: add the constraint to the objective, weighted by an unknown factor, then differentiate as if unconstrained. The factor is normally solved for at the end; here we never need to, because it is the ridge constant. Naming it \(1/k\) up front is why step 6 tidies so cleanly — and it also explains the direction of the relationship: a small \(k\) means a large multiplier, so the constraint dominates and the solution stays close to \(\hat\beta\).
What ridge really isAmong all coefficient vectors that explain the data equally well, ridge picks the shortest one. Occam's razor, written as algebra.

The equivalent dual form, equation (3.7), is the one modern textbooks use: minimize the error subject to \(\|B\|^2 \le R^2\). Same solutions, with \(k\) and \(R\) in one-to-one correspondence. Picture it in two dimensions: ellipses centered at \(\hat\beta\), a circle centered at the origin, and the ridge solution sitting exactly where the smallest ellipse touches the circle.

Is that tangency exact, or just a picture? Exact. At \(\hat\beta^{*}\), the gradient of the error is \(X'X(\hat\beta^{*} - \hat\beta) = -k\hat\beta^{*}\), which is anti-parallel to the gradient of \(\|B\|^2\), namely \(2\hat\beta^{*}\). Parallel gradients is precisely the condition for two level sets to be tangent.

Section 4 · paper §4, Theorems 4.1–4.3

The main theorem

We now have an estimator and a reason to like it. What remains is the real question: is it actually better? Section 4 of the paper answers yes, and proves it.

The plan: split the ridge estimator's total error into a variance part and a bias part, show one always falls and the other always rises, then compare their behavior near \(k = 0\).

Derivation · variance plus squared bias (4.2)–(4.6)
  1. start from \(\hat\beta^{*} = Z\hat\beta\) and add and subtract \(Z\beta\): \(\;\hat\beta^{*} - \beta = Z\hat\beta - Z\beta + Z\beta - \beta\)
  2. \(\;= \underbrace{Z(\hat\beta - \beta)}_{\text{random, mean }0} \;+\; \underbrace{(Z - I)\beta}_{\text{fixed}}\)
  3. square and take expectations. The cross term carries \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\beta - \beta] = 0\), so it vanishes and the two parts simply add:
  4. \(\mathbb{E}[L_1^2(k)] = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\|Z(\hat\beta-\beta)\|^2}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\|(Z-I)\beta\|^2}_{\text{bias}^2}\)
  5. the variance part. \(Z(\hat\beta-\beta)\) has mean zero and covariance \(Z\,\mathrm{Var}(\hat\beta)\,Z' = \sigma^2 Z(X'X)^{-1}Z'\); for a mean-zero vector, \(\mathbb{E}\|v\|^2 = \mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{Cov}\,v)\)
  6. so it equals \(\sigma^2\,\mathrm{tr}\big(Z(X'X)^{-1}Z'\big)\). In the eigenvector basis \(Z = PDP'\) with \(D = \mathrm{diag}\big(\tfrac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i+k}\big)\) and \((X'X)^{-1} = P\Lambda^{-1}P'\), so the product is \(P\,D\Lambda^{-1}D\,P'\)
  7. its trace is the sum of that diagonal: \(\;\gamma_1(k) = \sigma^2\displaystyle\sum_i \Big(\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i+k}\Big)^{2}\frac{1}{\lambda_i} = \sigma^2\sum_i \frac{\lambda_i}{(\lambda_i+k)^2}\)
  8. the bias part. First simplify \(Z - I\). Write \(X'X = (X'X + kI) - kI\), so \(\;Z = (X'X+kI)^{-1}\big[(X'X+kI) - kI\big] = I - k(X'X+kI)^{-1}\)
  9. hence \(Z - I = -k(X'X+kI)^{-1}\), and \(\;\|(Z-I)\beta\|^2 = k^2\beta'(X'X+kI)^{-2}\beta\)
  10. in the eigenvector basis, with \(\alpha = P'\beta\): \(\;\gamma_2(k) = k^2\displaystyle\sum_i \frac{\alpha_i^2}{(\lambda_i+k)^2}\)
  11. \(\;\;\mathbb{E}[L_1^2(k)] = \gamma_1(k) + \gamma_2(k)\) — equation (4.5)
Check the two ends before going on. At \(k = 0\): every shrink factor is 1, so \(\gamma_1 = \sigma^2\sum 1/\lambda_i\) — exactly the least squares error from Section 1 — while \(\gamma_2 = 0\) because of the leading \(k^2\). At the other extreme \(k \to \infty\): \(\gamma_1 \to 0\) and \(\gamma_2 \to \sum\alpha_i^2 = \beta'\beta\), since the estimate has been crushed to zero and the whole true vector is now error. The formula therefore interpolates between "all variance, no bias" and "no variance, all bias".

Now differentiate both pieces.

Derivation · monotonicity and the slopes at the origin
  1. differentiate \(\gamma_1\) term by term. Each term is \(\sigma^2\lambda_i(\lambda_i+k)^{-2}\), and \(\lambda_i\) is constant, so the power rule gives \(\sigma^2\lambda_i\cdot(-2)(\lambda_i+k)^{-3}\):
  2. \(\gamma_1'(k) = -2\sigma^2\displaystyle\sum_i \frac{\lambda_i}{(\lambda_i+k)^3} \;\lt\; 0\) — the variance is always decreasing (Theorem 4.1)
  3. now \(\gamma_2\). Each term is \(\alpha_i^2\,k^2(\lambda_i+k)^{-2}\); by the product rule \(\;2k(\lambda_i+k)^{-2} + k^2\cdot(-2)(\lambda_i+k)^{-3}\)
  4. factor out \(2k(\lambda_i+k)^{-3}\): \(\;2k(\lambda_i+k)^{-3}\big[(\lambda_i+k) - k\big] = 2k\lambda_i(\lambda_i+k)^{-3}\)
  5. \(\gamma_2'(k) = 2k\displaystyle\sum_i \frac{\lambda_i\alpha_i^2}{(\lambda_i+k)^3} \;\ge\; 0\) — the squared bias is always increasing (Theorem 4.2)
  6. now evaluate both at the origin. \(\;\gamma_1'(0) = -2\sigma^2\sum_i 1/\lambda_i^2\), which is enormously negative when any \(\lambda_i\) is small (4.9)
  7. \(\;\gamma_2'(0) = 0\), killed by the leading factor \(k\) (4.10)
The last two lines are the ones that matter, and they are worth stating in words. At \(k=0\) the bias curve leaves the origin flat — its slope is exactly zero — while the variance curve leaves with a steep downward slope, made steeper by precisely the small eigenvalues that were causing trouble. The first increment of shrinkage therefore buys variance at no cost in bias. Everything in the next derivation is bookkeeping around that observation.
Why not just do this in the original coordinates? Because the same quantity written there is unreadable. For the smallest interesting case — two predictors with correlation \(\rho\) — the variance term alone comes out as \[\gamma_1 = \frac{-2\sigma^2\Big(k\rho^2(\rho^2-1) - (k-\rho^2+1)(k\rho^2-k+\rho^2-1)\Big)}{(\rho^2-1)\,(k^2+2k-\rho^2+1)^2}\] and the squared bias is a comparable fraction in \(\beta_1, \beta_2, \rho, k\). Their derivative, which is what Theorem 4.3 actually needs, runs to some seven hundred characters. Is it positive or negative? You cannot tell by looking, and there is no term to examine separately — it is one indivisible expression. For \(p\) predictors rather than two it is worse still, with no form worth writing down.

In eigen coordinates the same thing is \(\;\gamma_1 + \gamma_2 = \sum_i \Big[\sigma^2\tfrac{\lambda_i}{(\lambda_i+k)^2} + k^2\tfrac{\alpha_i^2}{(\lambda_i+k)^2}\Big]\), a sum of \(p\) copies of one simple term, and each differentiates on its own to \[\frac{d}{dk}\left[\frac{\sigma^2\lambda_i + k^2\alpha_i^2}{(\lambda_i+k)^2}\right] = \frac{2\lambda_i\big(k\alpha_i^2 - \sigma^2\big)}{(\lambda_i+k)^3}\] whose sign you can read off at a glance: the denominator is positive, so everything hinges on whether \(k\alpha_i^2\) is below \(\sigma^2\). That single readable fact is the existence theorem. The change of coordinates is not decoration — without it there is no proof.
The asymmetry that proves everythingAt \(k = 0\) the variance is falling steeply while the bias curve is perfectly flat. So the total error must decrease as you start shrinking. The first bit of bias is free.

Making that precise gives the paper's headline result.

Derivation · the existence theorem (Theorem 4.3, eqs 4.15–4.16)
  1. add the two derivatives over a common denominator \((\lambda_i+k)^3\):
  2. \(\dfrac{d}{dk}\mathbb{E}[L_1^2(k)] = -2\sigma^2\displaystyle\sum_i \frac{\lambda_i}{(\lambda_i+k)^3} + 2k\sum_i \frac{\lambda_i\alpha_i^2}{(\lambda_i+k)^3}\)
  3. \(\;= \displaystyle\sum_i \frac{2\lambda_i\big(k\alpha_i^2 - \sigma^2\big)}{(\lambda_i + k)^3}\) — equation (4.15)
  4. every \(\lambda_i \gt 0\) and every \((\lambda_i+k)^3 \gt 0\), so each term's sign is decided entirely by the bracket \(k\alpha_i^2 - \sigma^2\)
  5. that bracket is negative exactly when \(k \lt \sigma^2/\alpha_i^2\); to make every term negative at once we need the strictest of those, namely \(k \lt \sigma^2/\alpha^2_{\max}\)
  6. so on the whole interval \(0 \lt k \lt \sigma^2/\alpha^2_{\max}\) the derivative is strictly negative, meaning \(\mathbb{E}[L_1^2]\) is strictly decreasing there
  7. a function that decreases on an interval is smaller at the far end than at the start: \(\;\;\mathbb{E}[L_1^2(k)] \lt \mathbb{E}[L_1^2(0)]\) for every such \(k\) — equation (4.16)
And such a \(k\) always exists, because \(\sigma^2 \gt 0\) makes the interval \(\big(0,\ \sigma^2/\alpha^2_{\max}\big)\) non-empty — for any \(X\), any \(\beta\), any noise level. That is Theorem 4.3, and it is the sentence the paper is remembered for: least squares is never the best you can do. Note what the bound is made of, though: \(\sigma^2\) and \(\alpha_{\max}\) are both unknown in practice, which is why the theorem proves existence without handing you a recipe. Section 5 is about that gap.

Read the condition as a rule of thumb: you can shrink safely until \(k\) times the largest squared canonical coefficient reaches the noise variance. Large true coefficients tolerate less shrinkage; noisy data tolerates more.

If a better \(k\) always exists, why isn't this automatic? Because the bound contains \(\alpha\) and \(\sigma^2\), which are unknown — they are what we were trying to estimate. The theorem guarantees existence, not a recipe. The paper is candid about this in §4c, and closing the gap is the subject of the next section.
Is ridge consistent as \(n\) grows? With \(k\) held fixed, no: \(\gamma_2\) does not vanish. In practice \(k\) is chosen from the data and shrinks with \(n\), which restores consistency. This is not in the paper — it is a good discussion point.

Section 5 · paper §3a, §5, §7

Choosing \(k\)

The paper's practical proposal is a diagnostic plot called the ridge trace: solve for \(\hat\beta^{*}(k)\) across a range of \(k\) and plot every coefficient as a curve against \(k\).

Here is a real one, from four predictors where the first two are near-copies of each other, with true coefficients \((1.5,\ 1.5,\ 1.0,\ -1.0)\):

\(k\)\(\hat\beta_1^{*}\)\(\hat\beta_2^{*}\)\(\hat\beta_3^{*}\)\(\hat\beta_4^{*}\)
0 (OLS)−0.55+3.49+1.26−1.18
0.05+1.16+1.68+1.18−1.21
0.30+1.22+1.31+0.94−1.19
1.00+1.01+1.03+0.59−1.00
truth+1.5+1.5+1.0−1.0

Look at the first row. Plain least squares returns \(-0.55\) for a coefficient whose true value is \(+1.5\) — the wrong sign entirely — while its correlated twin overshoots to \(+3.49\). By \(k = 0.05\) both have snapped into place, and from there the estimates drift slowly and predictably.

Hoerl and Kennard's advice (§7) is to pick the smallest \(k\) at which all four of these hold:

  1. The system has stabilized — the curves have flattened, and it behaves like an orthogonal system.
  2. Coefficients have reasonable absolute values for the quantities they represent.
  3. Coefficients that had obviously wrong signs at \(k = 0\) have corrected.
  4. The residual sum of squares has not inflated unreasonably: \(\phi^{*}(k) = \phi_{\min} + k^2\hat\beta^{*\prime}(X'X)^{-1}\hat\beta^{*}\).

The paper also derives a theoretical optimum in §5: per canonical direction, the best constant is \(k_i = \sigma^2/\alpha_i^2\), which can be estimated as \(\hat k_i = \hat\sigma^2/\hat\alpha_i^2\) and iterated until stable.

What we do todayIn 1970 this was a graphical, judgment-based choice. Today we pick \(k\) by cross-validation: split the data, try many values, keep the one that predicts held-out data best. The logic — trade bias for variance — is unchanged; only the selection was automated.

Section 6 · paper §6, §8

Connections and legacy

Ridge is a Bayesian estimate

Suppose that, before seeing data, you believe the coefficients are probably small — say \(\beta \sim N(0, \tau^2 I)\). Combine that belief with the data and maximize:

Derivation · prior to penalty
  1. Bayes' rule: the posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior, \(\;p(\beta \mid Y) \propto p(Y \mid \beta)\,p(\beta)\)
  2. the likelihood comes from \(Y \sim N(X\beta, \sigma^2 I)\): \(\;p(Y\mid\beta) \propto \exp\!\big(-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|Y - X\beta\|^2\big)\)
  3. the prior comes from \(\beta \sim N(0, \tau^2 I)\): \(\;p(\beta) \propto \exp\!\big(-\tfrac{1}{2\tau^2}\|\beta\|^2\big)\)
  4. multiply and take logs, which turns the product into a sum: \(\;\log p(\beta\mid Y) = \text{const} - \tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\|Y-X\beta\|^2 - \tfrac{1}{2\tau^2}\|\beta\|^2\)
  5. maximising that is the same as minimising its negative, and multiplying by \(2\sigma^2\) changes nothing about where the minimum sits:
  6. minimise \(\;\|Y - X\beta\|^2 + \dfrac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}\|\beta\|^2\) — which is exactly the penalised form of ridge, with \(k = \sigma^2/\tau^2\)
  7. to confirm, differentiate: \(\;-2X'(Y - X\beta) + 2\tfrac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}\beta = 0\)
  8. \(\Big(X'X + \dfrac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}I\Big)\beta = X'Y\), whose solution is \(\hat\beta^{*}\) with \(\;k = \sigma^2/\tau^2\)
So \(k\) is a ratio of two variances: the noise in the data over the spread you expected the coefficients to have. A firm prior belief that coefficients are small means a small \(\tau^2\) and therefore a large \(k\); a vague prior \((\tau^2 \to \infty)\) sends \(k \to 0\) and returns plain least squares. Read this way ridge is not a computational patch at all — it is the exact answer to a slightly different question, one in which you admit up front that huge coefficients were never plausible.

Where the idea went

  • Stein shrinkage. Stein (1956) and James–Stein (1961) showed that shrinking any estimate of three or more means toward zero improves mean squared error. Ridge is the regression member of that family, and the paper cites the connection in §6.
  • L2 regularization. Writing the dual form of Section 3 as a penalty, \(\min \|Y-X\beta\|^2 + k\|\beta\|^2\), gives the phrase you will see everywhere in machine learning.
  • Weight decay. The same \(k\|\theta\|^2\) penalty applied to neural network parameters. Every network trained with weight decay is running Hoerl and Kennard's recipe on millions of coefficients.
  • The lasso. Tibshirani (1996) replaced \(\|\beta\|^2\) with \(\sum_j|\beta_j|\). The constraint region becomes a diamond instead of a circle, the solution lands on a corner, and coefficients become exactly zero — variable selection, done properly. That is the natural next paper.
The lasting lessonUnbiased does not mean accurate. When your predictors are correlated, the celebrated least squares estimator can be nearly useless, and a small deliberate bias buys an enormous reduction in variance. Fifty-plus years later, that trade is the default setting of nearly every model we train.

Section 7

Exercises

These are ordered from confirming you followed the derivations to genuinely extending them.

1. Check the eigenvalues by hand

For \(X'X = \begin{pmatrix}1 & \rho\\ \rho & 1\end{pmatrix}\), verify that the eigenvalues are \(1\pm\rho\) with eigenvectors \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)\) and \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)\). Then confirm they sum to \(p = 2\) for any \(\rho\).

Hint

Apply the matrix to each candidate vector and check you get a multiple of it: \(\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1+\rho\\1+\rho\end{pmatrix}\).

2. Confirm the error formula numerically

Fix a correlated \(X\). Draw \(\varepsilon\) many times, compute \(\hat\beta\) each time, and average \(\|\hat\beta - \beta\|^2\). Compare with \(\sigma^2\sum_i 1/\lambda_i\) from equation (1.8). They should agree to Monte-Carlo accuracy.

3. Reproduce the paper's Figure 1

For your own \(X\) and \(\beta\), plot \(\gamma_1(k)\), \(\gamma_2(k)\) and their sum over \(k\in[0,1]\). Confirm visually that the sum dips below its value at \(k=0\) before rising, and check where the minimum falls relative to \(\sigma^2/\alpha^2_{\max}\).

4. Plot a ridge trace

Using Ridge(alpha=k) from scikit-learn over a grid of \(k\), reproduce a table like the one in Section 5 and apply the four criteria from §7. Try to construct a dataset where a coefficient starts with the wrong sign.

5. Extend the theorem

The existence theorem is about the error in the coefficients, \(\|\hat\beta^{*}-\beta\|^2\). Does the same argument work for prediction error, \(\|X\hat\beta^{*} - X\beta\|^2\)?

Hint

Prediction error introduces an extra factor of \(X'X\) in the middle, which replaces \((X'X)^{-1}\) by \(I\) in the eigen-sums. Redo steps 4 and 6 of the Section 4 derivation and see whether the slopes at \(k=0\) still have opposite behavior.

6. Shrink somewhere else

Ridge shrinks toward zero, which encodes the belief that coefficients are probably small. Suppose instead you believe \(\beta\) is near some known \(\beta_0\). Modify equation (2.1) accordingly, and check your answer against the Bayesian derivation in Section 6 with prior \(N(\beta_0, \tau^2I)\).
